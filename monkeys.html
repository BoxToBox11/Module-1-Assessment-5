MONKEYS

Monkeys are primates of the infraorder Simiiformes, belonging to various families within the order Primates. Two main groups are traditionally recognised, the New World monkeys (Platyrrhini) and the Old World monkeys (Catarrhini), with genetic evidence dividing them into distinct families like Cebidae, Cercopithecidae, and Callitrichidae. Monkeys are scattered throughout tropical regions of South America, Central America, Africa, and Asia. Simiiformes is the infraorder of simians; other, still surviving, members of this group include apes and humans. Mandrills are the largest extant monkeys and can reach a shoulder height of 0.5 m (1.6 ft) and weigh up to 36 kg (80 lb).

All monkeys have several distinctive features, the most notable of which is a long tail, used for balance and, in many New World species, as a prehensile limb for grasping objects and branches. Their forward-facing eyes give them excellent depth perception, serving as vital tools for navigating forest canopies. Monkeys' highly mobile shoulders and limbs help them swing and leap through trees. Their fur coats help to control their body temperature in diverse climates. New World monkeys have flat noses with side-facing nostrils, while Old World monkeys have narrow noses with downward-facing nostrils.

Monkeys are omnivorous or herbivorous and can be found in different habitats including rainforests, woodlands, savannahs, and mountainous terrains. They prefer to stay near areas with high tree cover or rocky outcroppings.
