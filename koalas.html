KOALAS

Koalas are arboreal marsupials of the family Phascolarctidae and the order Diprotodontia. Only one species is recognised, the koala (Phascolarctos cinereus), though regional populations vary slightly in size and fur thickness. Koalas are scattered throughout the eucalypt woodlands of eastern and southeastern Australia. Phascolarctidae is a family of marsupials where the koala is the only surviving member; other, now extinct, members of the family include prehistoric giant koalas. Adult male southern koalas are the largest of the species and can reach a body length of 85 cm (33 in) and weigh up to 15 kg (33 lb).

All koalas have several distinctive features, the most notable of which is a large, leathery black nose, used for sniffing out specific eucalyptus leaves. Their hands have two opposable thumbs, which serve as tools for climbing trees and grasping branches. Koalas' large, fluffy ear flaps help them catch sounds across their forest habitats. Their thick, padded fur on their bottoms helps them sit comfortably on rough branches for hours. Southern koalas have larger bodies and thicker, fluffier fur while northern koalas have smaller bodies and shorter, lighter grey fur.

Koalas are herbivorous specialists and can be found in different habitats including eucalyptus forests, woodlands, and coastal islands. They prefer to stay near water-rich eucalyptus trees which provide both food and hydration.
