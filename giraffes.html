GIRAFFES

Giraffes are tall mammals of the family Giraffidae and the order Artiodactyla. While traditionally recognised as a single species (Giraffa camelopardalis) with multiple subspecies, modern genetic evidence suggests there are four distinct species of giraffe: the northern, southern, reticulated, and Masai giraffe. Giraffes are scattered throughout sub-Saharan Africa. Giraffidae is a small family, with the okapi being the only other surviving member. Male giraffes are the tallest extant terrestrial animals and can reach a height of up to 5.7 m (19 ft) and weigh 1,900 kg (4,200 lb).

All giraffes have several distinctive features, the most notable of which is an extremely long neck, used for browsing leaves in high tree canopies and for territorial sparring. Both sexes have horn-like structures called ossicones, which are covered in skin and fur. Giraffes' large, prehensile, purplish-black tongues help them grasp leaves while avoiding sharp thorns. Their long, slender legs and large hooves carry their immense weight. Different species have unique coat patterns ranging from large, blocky spots to jagged, leaf-like designs against a cream background.

Giraffes are herbivorous browsers and can be found in different habitats including savannahs, woodlands, and shrublands. They prefer to stay near areas rich in acacia trees.
